# Careers in Cyber: Complete TryHackMe Walkthrough

Official room: https://tryhackme.com/room/careersincyber

## Room Overview & Information

**Category:** Cybersecurity careers and awareness  
**Difficulty:** Beginner  
**Objective:** Understand the range of cybersecurity careers, common entry routes, why the work matters, and how to choose a starting point.

This is a career-awareness room, not a technical exploitation challenge. Reconnaissance, initial access, and privilege escalation are not applicable. The correct workflow is to read each question, compare the options with the room's explanation, and distinguish a fact about the profession from an overconfident generalisation.

## Phase 1: Reconnaissance & Enumeration of Career Options

Before choosing a path, identify what different cyber roles actually do. Offensive security tests systems under authorization. Defensive security monitors, investigates, and responds to threats. Security engineering builds and operates protective controls. Governance, risk, and compliance teams turn requirements into policy and evidence. Digital forensics reconstructs events from artifacts and logs.

The room's message is that people can enter cybersecurity through multiple routes. Prior experience in teaching, customer service, healthcare, finance, systems administration, or other areas can transfer into security because the work needs communication, investigation, risk judgement, and technical knowledge.

## Phase 2: Understand the Profession

### Task 1: The Open Door

The task contrasts a claimed need for a computer-science degree with the fact that cybersecurity has roles for people from diverse backgrounds.

**Question:** “Which of these is true?”

Options include the claims that there are already enough professionals, that a computer-science degree is mandatory, or that many roles remain unfilled and people from different backgrounds can contribute.

**Answer:** Millions of roles are unfilled, and people from all backgrounds can fill them.

This is the intended learning point: one degree is not a universal prerequisite. It does not mean every role is easy to enter; skills and experience still need to be developed and demonstrated.

### Task 2: What It Pays

Pay varies by geography, organisation, specialism, experience, and responsibility. Certifications can help demonstrate knowledge, but they do not guarantee a particular salary or job.

**Question:** “Which of these is true?”

The intended choice is the statement that certifications can help people progress toward well-paying cybersecurity work faster than they may expect.

**Answer:** Certifications alone can get you to a well-paying role, faster than most people expect.

Interpret this as encouragement to learn and validate skills, not a promise that a certificate on its own guarantees employment. Employers commonly assess practical ability, communication, judgement, and evidence of real work.

### Task 3: The Bigger Picture

Security incidents can affect hospitals, banks, power grids, factories, government services, and individual privacy. Impact can include loss of availability, exposure of sensitive data, financial damage, and threats to physical safety.

**Question:** “Which of these is true?”

**Answer:** Cyber professionals protect hospitals, power grids, factories, and much more.

The task corrects the misconception that cyber incidents only damage technology companies or company profits.

### Task 4: Find Your Place

Cybersecurity includes penetration testing, SOC monitoring, threat intelligence, incident response, forensics, cloud security, application security, security engineering, and governance.

**Question:** “Which of these is true?”

**Answer:** There are many roles in cyber security, and the right one depends on how your mind works.

Choose based on the work you enjoy and can practise. Investigative thinkers may enjoy DFIR; people who like software may enjoy application security; those who like building systems may prefer engineering; and those who like monitoring and triage may prefer SOC operations.

### Task 5: Your Starting Point

New technologies introduce new assets and threats. Cloud services, AI systems, connected healthcare, digital banking, and industrial automation all require people who can define security requirements, validate system behaviour, handle incidents, and make risk decisions.

**Question:** “Which of these is true?”

**Answer:** Cyber security is one of the fastest growing fields, and every new technology expands it further.

The intended answer is about the continuing need for security as systems change. It is not a guarantee of employment or a claim that hiring conditions are identical in every region.

### Task 6: Launch Careers Quiz

Open the room's **Launch Careers Quiz** and answer based on the work you find interesting. Treat the result as a suggestion for exploration, not a formal aptitude test or a permanent assignment.

Questions worth asking yourself include:
- Do you prefer investigation, programming, communication, systems administration, or research?
- Do you like responding to active incidents or preventing problems through design?
- Do you enjoy broad context or deep technical specialisation?
- Which skills can you demonstrate today, and what should you learn next?
- Which local training, internship, and entry-level opportunities are available?

## Phase 3: Practical Skill-Building and Professional Practice

No exploit path or privilege escalation is part of the room. Convert the career reflection into one small portfolio project. A beginner interested in defence can analyse a sample log, write a detection query, and document the evidence. Someone interested in web security can learn HTTP and authentication, then practise in authorised labs. A prospective engineer can build a small application and document how access control and secrets are handled.

For every project, record the objective, environment, steps, evidence, conclusion, and remediation. Remove credentials and private data from public notes. A small completed project with a clear explanation is more valuable than a long list of tools that have never been used.

## Phase 5: Key Takeaways & Remediation

Cybersecurity is not a single job and does not have one universal educational route. Build core skills, select a track based on interests, practise in authorised environments, document findings clearly, and keep learning as technology changes. Certifications can help structure study, but practical competence and communication remain essential.
